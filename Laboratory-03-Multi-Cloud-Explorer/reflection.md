1. Which cloud platforms impressed you the most?
 Of the major cloud providers I explored, AWS impressed me the most. What stood out first was the sheer scale of its service catalog from EC2 and S3 to more specialized tools like Lambda for serverless computing and RDS for managed databases. Having that much depth in one ecosystem made it possible to build fairly sophisticated architectures without ever leaving the platform. What really solidified my impression, though, was AWS's Identity and Access Management (IAM) system. Compared to the access controls I'd seen elsewhere, IAM felt remarkably granular I could scope permissions down to specific actions on specific resources, which made the principle of least privilege feel achievable rather than theoretical. That level of control clarified for me how serious enterprise-grade security actually gets implemented in practice, not just discussed in theory. AWS's documentation and community support also played a role in shaping my impression. Because it's been the market leader for so long, almost every problem I encountered had already been solved by someone else, with a clear walkthrough available. That maturity reduced friction significantly when I was troubleshooting configuration issues or trying to understand a new service for the first time. None of this is to say the other platforms lacked strengths Azure's enterprise integration and GCP's data tooling were both genuinely impressive in their own right. But AWS's combination of breadth, fine-grained control, and ecosystem maturity gave me the most confidence that I was learning transferable, industry-relevant skills.

2.What similarities exist among AWS, Azure and GCP? 
Despite their different branding, pricing quirks, and console layouts, AWS, Azure, and GCP share a surprising amount of common architectural DNA which makes sense, since they're all solving the same fundamental problem of delivering computing resources over the internet. At the core, all three offer the same basic service categories. Each has a flagship compute service EC2 on AWS, Virtual Machines on Azure, and Compute Engine on GCP that lets users spin up virtual servers on demand. Each also provides object storage (S3, Blob Storage, and Cloud Storage respectively) for storing unstructured data at scale, along with managed relational database services so teams don't have to administer database servers themselves. Identity and access management is another shared pillar. All three platforms let administrators define granular permissions, roles, and policies to control who can access which resources reflecting a shared industry standard around the principle of least privilege. Pricing models are similarly aligned: all three operate on a pay-as-you-go basis, charging for actual resource consumption rather than requiring large upfront hardware investments, and all three offer free tiers to let newcomers experiment before committing financially. This convergence suggests that despite competing for market share, all three providers are responding to the same customer demands: flexibility, scalability, reduced operational overhead, and a clear path toward cloud-native application design.

3.How do business requirements influence cloud platform selection?
Working through this project made clear that choosing a cloud platform is rarely a purely technical decision it's fundamentally a business decision shaped by an organization's specific constraints and priorities. Cost structure is often the first filter. A startup with limited runway might prioritize a generous free tier or lower compute costs, while an enterprise with predictable workloads might negotiate committed-use discounts that only make sense at scale. Pricing models that look similar on the surface can produce very different total costs depending on usage patterns. Existing vendor relationships matter just as much. An organization already deep in the Microsoft ecosystem using Active Directory, Office 365, and on-premise Windows Server infrastructure will find Azure's integration advantages hard to ignore, even if a competitor offers a marginally cheaper service elsewhere. Switching costs and staff familiarity are real business factors, not just technical ones. Compliance and regulatory requirements also narrow the field quickly. A healthcare company bound by HIPAA or a government contractor requiring FedRAMP authorization needs a provider that can demonstrably meet those standards, which immediately rules out platforms that can't. Team expertise plays a role too the "best" platform on paper is worth little if a team lacks the skills to deploy and maintain it securely. Finally, latency and geographic requirements matter for user-facing applications; a company serving customers concentrated in one region will weigh a provider's data center footprint there heavily. Ultimately, platform selection isn't about which cloud is objectively superior it's about the closest fit to the business's actual constraints.

4.What did you learn from acting as a cloud Solutions Consultant?
Stepping into the role of a cloud solutions consultant taught me that technical expertise alone isn't enough the real skill lies in translating that expertise into recommendations a business can actually act on. One of the biggest lessons was that clients don't think in terms of services and APIs; they think in terms of outcomes, budgets, and risk. Recommending a particular architecture wasn't just about picking the most powerful or elegant solution it meant asking questions first: What's the budget? What compliance requirements apply? What's the team's existing skill set? A technically "perfect" solution that ignores those constraints isn't actually a good recommendation. I also learned the importance of communicating trade-offs clearly rather than presenting a single option as the obvious answer. Different stakeholders care about different things a CFO wants to understand cost predictability, while an engineering lead wants to know about scalability and maintainability. Learning to frame the same technical decision in language that resonates with different audiences was a skill I hadn't fully appreciated before this exercise. Another key takeaway was the value of justifying decisions with concrete reasoning rather than platform loyalty or personal preference. Instead of defaulting to "AWS because it's the market leader," I had to articulate why a specific service fit a specific business scenario. Overall, this experience reinforced that consulting is as much about listening and diagnosing real needs as it is about technical knowledge and that the best recommendations are the ones tailored to the client's actual situation, not a generic best practice.

5.How has your GitHub Cloud Computing Portfolio Improved after completing this mission?
Completing this mission pushed my GitHub portfolio from a scattered collection of isolated scripts into something that actually resembles the kind of structured, professional work employers look for. Before this project, my repositories tended to be minimal code with little context, no explanation of the problem being solved, and no documentation of the architecture behind it. Now, each project includes a proper README that walks through the purpose of the deployment, the services used, and the reasoning behind key design decisions. That shift alone makes the work far more legible to anyone reviewing it who wasn't there for the process. I also started incorporating architecture diagrams to visually represent how different cloud components interact compute, storage, networking, and IAM roles laid out so a reviewer can understand the system at a glance rather than having to trace it through code. That kind of visual communication is something I hadn't prioritized before, but it's clearly expected in real-world cloud engineering documentation. Beyond documentation, the actual content of my repositories evolved too. I moved from manually clicking through console configurations toward Infrastructure as Code practices, capturing deployments in reusable templates rather than one-off manual setups. That shift demonstrates a more mature, repeatable approach to cloud work. Altogether, my portfolio now reflects not just that I can make something function in the cloud, but that I understand how to plan, document, and present cloud architecture the way a working professional would which feels like a meaningful step toward job readiness.
